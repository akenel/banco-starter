0 · Establish this first

it decides section 1
Does the shop have a Worldline terminal at the counter TODAY, or does it arrive with go-live? Everything below assumes the terminal produces its own daily settlement, itemised by scheme, which is where the real card breakdown comes from. If the terminal is not in yet, the paper breakdown is genuinely the only one they have and the button decision changes. Ask before deciding anything in section 1.

Second question, same conversation, and it costs him nothing to answer: “What do people actually pay with in your shop — cash, card, TWINT, roughly what split?” He knows. It beats guessing, and it is the kind of question that makes him feel asked rather than sold to.

1 · The payment buttons

his complaint, and he is right

Felix: “too many buttons.” Four tenders today — Cash, Visa, Debit, TWINT — and the cashier picks one, then does the Worldline step separately. He wants one button, cash or card.

The decision: Cash · Card · TWINT — three, not four, not two

tenderown button?why
CashyesThe only one that touches the physical drawer count.
TWINTyesThe terminal shows a different flow for it, so the cashier knows with certainty. It is also the tender Felix most wants tracked.
Visa vs DebitnoSame terminal, same settlement, same payout — and the cashier is guessing. Debit Mastercard looks like a Mastercard, Visa Debit looks like a Visa, a phone tapping shows her nothing.
The rule behind it: a tender earns its own button if and only if it changes where the money physically lands, or who you reconcile against at close — and the cashier can actually tell. Visa vs Debit fails all three.

The argument that wins it — and it is an accounting argument, not a screen one

Those buttons are not just feeding a screen. They feed rows in the Z-report and in the Banana CSV (Cash · TWINT · Visa · Debit · Bank transfer · Crypto · Other). So the honest framing is:

  • Today, when she taps “Visa” for a Visa Debit, both rows in the accounting file are wrong even though the sum is right — and the disagreement is unprovable, so end-of-day arguments have no resolution.
  • Merging them into one Card line makes the file more honest, because it stops asserting a distinction nobody can substantiate.
  • When Worldline is integrated, the terminal supplies the brand and the rows come back for real, with nobody tapping anything.

And make it a setting, so the argument does not need winning

One switch in Settings: ask the cashier for card type — on / off. On for the trial, so Felix keeps exactly the breakdown he has on paper today and loses nothing. If the taps slow Layla down, or he watches a shift and changes his mind, he flips it himself — no release, no argument. When Worldline goes in it goes off for good.

Keep the data model exactly as it is. The payment-type field stays capable of holding visa / mastercard / debit / twint. You are not deleting the capability, only no longer asking a human to guess it. No migration, no schema change, historic rows stay valid.

Normally “make it configurable” is how you get a settings swamp. This one earns it: a genuine buyer/builder disagreement, display-only, and a natural end state.

Downstream, so it is not a surprise: the receipt line Payment Method: VISA, the Z-report rows and the Banana CSV all read the same field. They move together with this decision.

2 · Worldline is Phase 2

and never say “when Worldline is in, the buttons go”

Two things are being bundled and must be separated:

  1. Recording that a sale was paid by card — payment type on the transaction, card total on the shift close. Not optional, in from day one, already built and simulated.
  2. Terminal automation — Banco pushes the amount, the terminal answers, the sale completes itself. Saves typing and a typo. An optimisation.
The argument that wins, and it is not about scope: an unintegrated terminal is a separate failure domain. If Banco is down, wedged or mid-restart, the shop still takes card, because the terminal is standalone and does not care. Integrate on day one and the shop’s ability to take money is coupled to the newest software in the building. Small shops run standalone terminals permanently, by choice, for exactly this reason.

Secondary but real: the lead time is Worldline’s, not yours — sandbox slots, a test terminal, their process — and scheduling 1 Oct behind a third party is how 1 Oct slips. And sandbox-green would not settle it anyway; it would be re-verified on the real terminal, on the shop’s network, at go-live. That is LESSON #1 with a card reader attached.

Consistent with a decision already made: the refund path hands back cash even on a card sale — “simpler for accounting”. That only makes sense in a world where the terminal is not driven by Banco. The design has been heading here already.

Name the cost before he finds it. Every evening someone ties the terminal’s own daily total to Banco’s card total. If they differ, someone hunts. It is a minute a day and he will feel it every single day. It is the strongest argument for integration and he will hit it in week one. Say it first — a skeptic believes your upside only after you have handed him the downside yourself.

What to say

“Card payments are in from day one — Banco records them, and the card total is on your shift close so you can tie it to the terminal’s own report. What I’m not doing yet is having Banco drive the terminal.

Reason: I don’t want your card payments depending on my software while my software is still new. Standalone, the terminal keeps working even if Banco doesn’t.

The cost is you tie two totals together each evening. It takes a minute. Trade with me: run four weeks. If that minute is annoying you, we do the Worldline integration then — and by then I’ll have their sandbox and a test terminal, and it’ll be a clean piece of work instead of a risk stacked on top of go-live.”

The sentence to avoid: “I’ll remove those buttons once Worldline is enabled.” That hands him the reply “fine — wire up Worldline now.” Never let the thing he dislikes be curable by the thing you don’t want to do yet. The buttons are a setting; the integration is a risk decision. Two separate conversations, said separately.

Take Worldline’s sandbox offer — and schedule it for after go-live. Declining reads as avoidance; scheduling reads as a plan, and it keeps the relationship warm for when you need it. Do not discuss what the integration costs on the day. It is a separate ticket, priced after a dry run — same rule as the go-live itself, and commercially better for you as its own line.

3 · Split tender

parked, and billable if he asks

Angel’s call, 4 Sep: “a nightmare waiting to happen — leave it, settle it later if it comes up.” Agreed. Literal half-cash-half-card is rare in ordinary retail and rarer in Switzerland. If Felix asks for it, it is a quoted change, not a defect.

But one form of it already exists and is not called split tender: Banco has member credits. A member with CHF 4.20 of credits against a CHF 12.00 sale is a two-tender sale whether or not it is named one. If that path is not clean, the cashier rings it full cash and adjusts the credits by hand — and the member ledger and the cash book quietly stop agreeing. Worth probing before the trial; it is the split tender you already shipped.

4 · The terms

agree these BEFORE the trial, not after

The plan is sound: release, one-month trial, changes billable. One word will cost money if it survives —

“I charge for every one unless it’s obviously my fault.”

“Obviously” will be litigated on every single item, by a perfectionist, for free, forever. You will not agree on where it sits and there is no rule to point at. Don’t fix it with a better adjective — fix it with the vocabulary already in the testsheets, which he has already used:

FAIL — I could not complete the task. That is a defect. Fixed free.
ISSUE — it worked, I want it different. That is a change. Quoted.

It is objective enough to survive an argument, and it puts the classification on the observation rather than on whose fault it was.

  • Write down what the trial is testing before it starts. Otherwise everything you did not build becomes “but I assumed it would,” and against an unbounded scope the perfectionist always wins. A perfectionist does not naturally emit a “done” signal — you have to define the finish line in advance.
  • Never give changes away by default. Price them, then discount visibly: “that’s about two hours, CHF X — first one’s on me.” Free-by-default teaches him the list costs nothing. A visible discount teaches him it has a price and you are being generous with it.
  • The “confirmed by” column is the acceptance record. Every fix, a name, a date, a build. Keep it running through the trial month — in October it is what you point at.
  • Go-live is the billable ticket, priced after a dry run, never quoted on the trial day.

5 · What to expect from him

so the silence is not misread

He will never say “excellent.” He is not evaluating the software; he is evaluating his own exposure — every button is a thing a cashier can do that he has to deal with later. That scan has no output for “good,” which is exactly why the VAT never gets a word. His approval is behavioural: he lets Layla near it, he stops proposing alternatives, he starts asking what it costs and when. Waiting for the sentence will burn a lot of energy for no information.

The pen-and-paper thing is not hypocrisy — it is the best brief he has given

Paper is forgiving because mistakes stay visible: scratched out, arrow drawn, correction readable by anyone, nothing hidden and nothing final. A computer removes both. So “idiot-proof” is not a request for polish — it is “I can see the mistake, and it can be undone.” That is a product requirement and it is a good one.

Be straight about the goal: “no way a cashier can screw up” is not achievable by anyone, including paper. “Every screw-up is visible and reversible” is — and that is the version worth promising, because you can deliver it.

Refunds and voids are manager-only (role-gated, with a test pinning it). That is the seam where he will say paper wins. Sell it as control, not friction:

“On paper, Layla scratches it out and you never know it happened. Here she has to call you — and you see every one. That’s not the system getting in her way, that’s you getting your eyes on it.”

How to run the meeting

  • Don’t show him screens. A screen is the only thing you have handed him to react to, so he reacts to the buttons. Show a scenario: Layla rings the wrong item — watch what happens. That is the question he is actually asking.
  • Give him a job: try to break it. A perfectionist with a destructive task is engaged and genuinely useful. A perfectionist with nothing to do audits your fonts.
  • Ask a closed question at the end — “can Layla run one shift on this?” — never “what do you think,” which is an open invitation to the list.
  • Lead with the scoreboard, not the raw list. A long unsorted ISSUE list reads as “this software is bad” even at 10 pass / 3 issue / 0 fail.
  • The 20 tiles are not hers. If “too many buttons” is aimed at the dashboard: “That screen is yours. Hers has five.” — Scan · Cart · Catalog · Customers · My Day.
  • Functionality that works is invisible. He will never notice the VAT fix by looking. If you want credit for it, you have to say it happened.
The uncomfortable one, and he may raise it: self-hosting swaps vendor risk for a bus factor of one, and that one is you. From where he sits, “the vendor might vanish” and “this guy might get ill or get busy” are the same risk in different clothes. A good part of his perfectionism is probably pricing that. Which means the runbook, the backups, the restore drill and the go-live kit are not hardening chores after the product — for Felix they are what makes it buyable. Decide whether that goes in the conversation out loud rather than hoping he doesn’t ask.

6 · Where Banco actually stands

an honest read, 5 Sep

Not in a different league on function for this vertical. Probably ahead of a generic install on what this shop does daily; behind on integration breadth and operational maturity.

aheadbehind
Age verification with an evidence record — refusals, attestations, audit trail. Most POS have a checkbox or nothing. Terminal integration — deliberate, but the most visible gap when a competitor demos beside you.
Four languages, natively, in a four-language country. Offline writes. P2 in sw.js’s own comments — the till browses offline and cannot finalise a sale.
Pooled tier pricing agreed across till, cart, kiosk and the held-orders board. Receipt and label printing, still being wired.
Banana CSV with the 5-rappen Rundungsdifferenz on its own line, giveaways as COGS, a VAT block that does not double-count on import. Most products dump a flat CSV. Support and continuity — the bus-factor point above.
Ownership. Nobody else in the market offers it at all.
The competitor to watch is not the top three. It is the bottom — SumUp and friends hand a small shop a reader and a free POS app. For a one-counter headshop, “free, works tomorrow, no setup” is far more dangerous than an enterprise product with a five-figure implementation. The answer to them is the age gate, the four languages, the tiers and the ownership — not polish, which you will not out-spend them on and do not need to.

On look and feel: the criticism you are getting is about polish, not usability — “too many buttons,” not “I can’t find anything.” You only earn the first kind after the second has stopped. The remaining gap against a mature product is consistency across forty screens, which is hours, not genius. And the bugs found this week — VAT only on pooled baskets, a keypad that fails on one browser, off-axis glare — are late-stage bugs. Toy projects never reach them, because nobody uses them hard enough.

8 · Not about Felix — the market question

research, not a pipeline

Walking into headshops and asking questions is the highest-value thing a solo founder can do, and it is not impolite. But the first ten visits answer one question, and it decides the next year: is Felix’s pain common, or is Felix’s pain Felix’s? Ten conversations settles it. A hundred is not needed.

  • Never ask “would you like one?” Hypotheticals produce polite yes-es that predict nothing. Ask about the past and the present, never the future.
  • Ask instead: “What do you use to ring things up?” · “When was the last time it annoyed you?” · “How do you do your end of day?” · “What do you do about the age check?” · “Who set it up — can you change anything yourself?” and the one that is the whole pitch: “If it broke tomorrow, who do you call?”
  • Open as a peer, not a vendor: “I built the till for a shop in Luzern — I’m trying to work out whether it’s useful to anybody else or specific to them.” Ask Felix before naming his shop, and never to a shop in the same city.
  • Mid-morning or the dead afternoon hour, weekday. Never Saturday. Don’t demo on visit one — listen, take a card, leave; the demo is visit two.
  • Write each visit up the same day, same questions, same order. Twenty recorded consistently is a dataset. Twenty remembered is a vibe.
  • The higher-leverage channel is the wholesaler whose feed is already integrated — their rep visits every one of these shops, knows the owners, and can say “the system that already knows our catalogue.” Same shape: CannaTrade and the trade fairs, fifty owners in one room who came to talk. Romandie and Ticino are servable but not walk-in-able without French — that is an argument for a distributor, not a write-off.
